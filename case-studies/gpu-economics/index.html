Case study

What would my AI platform cost to rent?

Independent R&D · Reference build · single-operator sandbox · November 2026

The GPU is only part of the picture. An AI platform also needs compute, storage, networking and recovery. I wanted to see the whole bill, not just the headline price of a GPU.

The question

For a self-hosted AI platform with two RTX PRO 5000 72 GB GPUs, I wanted a repeatable way to compare the local setup with a rented alternative. Sticker price alone could not answer it: interactive inference, batch experiments and always-on services have different availability needs.

I built a local daily report that reads sampled GPU utilization from monitoring data, combines it with configurable rental-rate assumptions and a purchase-cost input, and keeps a daily history. It compares estimated busy-time rental with always-on scenarios. The detailed report stays local; the figures below are selected aggregates from an earlier snapshot.

Measure first, then compare

The report counts a sampled interval as busy when utilization exceeds a threshold, and also reports utilization-weighted hours. Busy GPU-hours are a useful signal—not proof that a provider would bill for exactly those hours. A cloud GPU may need to be reserved while idle, and short requests, missing samples, startup time or different performance can change the comparison.

Important: purchase price divided by estimated rental cost is a simple gross payback proxy, not full ROI or realized savings. It does not show profit, and it is only meaningful when the capacity being compared can meet the same workload requirements.

The rental assumption in this snapshot uses a 96 GB GPU class, while the two installed cards have 72 GB each. That is not an equivalent-hardware quote. Prices, VM sizing, storage, networking and restore transfer are assumptions; they need provider quotes and a workload match before they can support a financial decision.

Data-quality check: a separate November 10 check found roughly 51% and 24% of expected five-minute samples for the two tracked GPUs over the preceding 15 days. The earlier snapshot's exact collection window has not been independently reconciled. Missing observations cannot be treated as idle time, so neither the snapshot's busy total nor its derived rental estimates should be treated as verified complete.

Before relying on a comparison, I need complete monitoring coverage, a verified net purchase cost, dated quotes for equivalent hardware and a workload-specific availability model. A reserved GPU may be billed even while idle.

Why always-on availability changes the comparison

For a service expected to respond at any time, the question is not just how long a GPU is busy—it is how long capacity must remain available. An earlier scenario assumed 2.2 busy GPU-hours per day of separate batch work in addition to the reserved serving capacity. It used a two-GPU purchase input of $18,244 and the same $1.09–$2.18 per GPU-hour rental range.

Efficiency is more than a rental rate

In an initial two-stream-per-card clock test, the lower clock setting produced roughly 148–152 tokens per second per card versus roughly 152 unlocked. Observed card power fell from 250 W to approximately 173–177 W, and temperatures fell by about 3–8 °C. These are preliminary results from one heavy-load test—not a guarantee for other models, request patterns or cooling conditions. Further clock settings and sustained testing are needed.

Heat, power, responsiveness and system availability belong in the decision alongside the rental estimate. Shared fleet services, backups and networking also need equivalent scope and costs on both sides; they should not be assumed identical without checking.

What the quick calculation misses

  • Ownership costs: electricity, cooling, host hardware, networking, maintenance, downtime, financing, depreciation and eventual replacement.
  • Rental costs and constraints: storage, data transfer, minimum billing periods, capacity availability and any required supporting services.
  • Real equivalence: model fit, memory, throughput, latency, concurrency, availability and operational effort—not just a similar GPU label.
  • Alternatives: resale value, existing sunk costs and whether the workload could use a smaller instance, API, shared service or a different schedule.

The current report explicitly excludes power, hosting and resale from its simple comparison. It is a starting point for a fuller total-cost model, not a financial decision on its own.

The takeaway

Measure utilization, model the availability you actually need, and make the assumptions visible. Local compute can offer control and predictable capacity; rental can offer flexibility and avoid an upfront purchase. Neither wins automatically.

For me, the value of the exercise is the discipline: turn a hardware debate into a workload-specific comparison, acknowledge the gaps, and revisit it as usage changes.

Details have been generalized. This is a dated independent R&D case study, not financial advice or a live cost dashboard.
